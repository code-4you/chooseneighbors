What makes it last · Part A

Governing
ourselves

A community survives or dies on how it handles its own friction. Choosing your neighbours is the start — but it isn't enough on its own. These are the four rules that keep a chosen community from turning on itself, being captured, seizing up, or rotting from the inside.

The four rules

  1. Resolving conflict without exile
  2. Rules that can't be captured
  3. Deciding together without deadlock
  4. Money everyone can see

It's tempting to think that if you simply pick good people to live near, the rest takes care of itself. It doesn't. Every community in history — from the Epicurean communes to the cohousing project down the road — has discovered that the warm parts only last if a few unglamorous things are handled first.

None of what follows is bureaucracy for its own sake. Each rule answers a specific, well-documented way that good communities fall apart. Get these right and everything you actually came for — the neighbours, the shared meals, the work you care about — finally has something solid to grow on.

01

Resolving conflict without exile

Prevents: a community that turns on its own members

The book's instinct is that if you don't get on with someone, one of you can move. That helps, and it matters — being trapped beside someone you dislike is one of the worst parts of how housing works now. But a community whose only tool is "make someone leave" eventually becomes anxious and brittle. People stop raising small problems for fear of where it leads.

The political economist Elinor Ostrom spent a career studying communities that govern shared resources for generations without collapsing. One feature shows up again and again: graduated, low-cost dispute resolution — a ladder of small, cheap steps people can climb before anything drastic happens. Repair comes first; removal is the last rung, not the first.

How it works here

  1. A quiet word. Most things end here — a direct conversation, with a trusted neighbour alongside if either person wants one.
  2. Facilitated mediation. A trained local volunteer sits with both people to find a workable agreement. No verdict, no blame.
  3. A council hearing. If it can't be settled, the local circle hears it and issues a clear, written outcome that both sides can see and refer back to.
  4. A required move — last resort only. And even then, never as punishment that leaves someone with nothing: their home's value is returned at the standard formula price, and they're helped to a new place.
02

Rules that can't be captured

Prevents: the community being seized from the inside

This is the most common way a project like this dies — and the book already saw it. At the Mahana Coromandel commune, the founding idea of free, un-owned land was, over the years, watered down by people who changed the rules to keep the land for themselves. The Epicurean communes became monasteries. The pattern is almost a law: open things tend to get quietly privatised by whoever is standing closest when the rules are loose.

The defence is constitutional. A small number of core promises are written as an entrenched charter — open space stays open, land can never be privately captured, no one pays rent, anyone can always move. These few rules are made deliberately very hard to change. Everything else stays flexible and local.

How it works here

The charter can't be altered by a single council on a single evening. Changing a core promise requires a large supermajority across multiple villages, plus a mandatory waiting period before any change takes effect — long enough that no momentary faction can capture it. Day-to-day rules, by contrast, each village sets and changes freely for itself. The charter is simply the firewall between a living community and someone's private estate.

03

Deciding together without deadlock

Prevents: both a strongman and endless argument

"People form their own council" is a good start, but it leaves out the hardest part: how a decision actually gets made. Skip that, and a group drifts toward one of two failures — a single forceful person quietly running everything, or meetings that never end because everyone must be enthusiastic before anything moves.

Most cohousing communities that thrive use a method called sociocracy, or consent-based decision-making. The shift is subtle and powerful: a proposal goes ahead unless someone raises a reasoned, good-faith objection — not when everyone is excited, which would freeze the place solid. Work is organised into small circles, each with a clear patch of responsibility.

How it works here

  1. Small circles. Housing, work, care, events — each is run by a small circle with a clear remit, so decisions sit close to the people they affect.
  2. Consent, not unanimity. A proposal moves forward when no one has a sound objection. Objections aren't vetoes to wield — they're problems the circle solves together.
  3. Open nomination, rotating roles. Organisers nominate themselves (as the book imagines), and roles are reviewed on a set cycle so no position quietly becomes permanent.
  4. Up the levels. Anything bigger than one circle rises to the village circle, and anything shared across villages to the federation circle.
04

Money everyone can see

Prevents: trust collapsing over hidden funds

Belonging pools money for real things — community ideas, helping inventors, backing local business, paying for care. The moment any of that becomes opaque, people assume the worst, and often they're right. Nothing erodes a community faster than the sense that someone, somewhere, is doing well out of the shared pot.

So this one is simple, and deliberately boring: the kind of boring that keeps a community alive — and exactly what councils and investors need to see before they'll take you seriously.

How it works here

  1. Open books. Every member can see income and every dollar spent, written in plain language — not buried in a report no one reads.
  2. Independent audit. The fund is checked by someone outside the community, on a regular cycle.
  3. Two hands on anything significant. Clear spending limits, and at least two signatories required above them. No single person moves real money alone.
  4. Decided in the open. Where the fund goes is settled by the same consent method as everything else — in view of the people whose money it is.

These four rules aren't the soul of Belonging — the neighbours and the shared life are. But they're the floor that soul stands on. A community that can repair conflict, resist capture, decide without seizing up, and account for its money openly is one that can still be here in fifty years, and in the lives that come after.

Self-government

You
design
it

You're not governed by strangers who'll never know you existed. Here, the people who live in a place run it — proposing ideas, solving local problems, organising the life of the area, and being funded to do it. The community is something you help shape, not something handed to you finished.

You can vote for a government you'll never meet. Or you can help design the place you actually live.

In an ordinary town, the rules and the rhythms are set somewhere far away, by people who don't know you and won't. You can write a letter, cast a vote, and mostly watch. If you have an idea for making your own street better, there's usually nowhere for it to go.

Belonging flips that. The smallest, most local layer of government is yours — close enough to actually change, by the people it affects. Not a feeling of having a say. A real one.

How it works

Anyone can step up; ideas have a real path; good ones get funded.

  1. Organisers step forward

    Anyone who wants to help can nominate themselves to organise part of community life — events, a project, a local problem worth solving. You don't wait to be chosen by a distant authority; you put your hand up for the patch you care about.

  2. Decisions made the sound way

    Work is organised into small circles, each with a clear remit, and decisions move forward by consent rather than by a strongman or endless argument. Roles rotate on a set cycle, so no position quietly becomes permanent. (The full method is in Governing ourselves.)

  3. An open index of ideas

    Every idea for the community lives in a shared, visible list — yours, your neighbours', and ones borrowed from other villages. Each group can browse it, adopt what fits, and design their own version of community life from it. Nothing good has to be reinvented, and nothing gets lost.

  4. Good ideas get backing

    Ideas that develop the community or solve a real local problem can be funded from the community pot — and even modestly paid, because they're things people want to do, not jobs done only for money. The books are open, so everyone sees where it goes. (See Governing ourselves.)

What "the index of ideas" might hold

A living menu each village draws from and adds to — the raw material of a community designing itself:

A weekly shared dinner A repair workshop An elders' check-in roster A makers' market A childcare co-op A local growing plot An ideas night A newcomers' welcome A tool library A performance series
Why it matters

A role in the place you live is one of the things people lose, and miss most.

Émile Durkheim observed that when people moved from villages into cities, they lost the roles and standing a community once gave them — and something in them suffered for it. Giving people, especially older and retired members, a real hand in shaping where they live isn't decoration. It's handing back a kind of meaning the modern city quietly took away.

This is the difference between living somewhere and belonging to it. When the place you live is partly your design — when your idea can become the thing that happens on Thursday night — you stop being a resident among strangers and become part of something that knows you're there.

The decision-making, the rotating roles, and the open money all live in Governing ourselves. How those roles become meaningful work is in Pulling our weight.

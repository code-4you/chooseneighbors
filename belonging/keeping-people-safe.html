Keeping
people safe

The same features that make Belonging powerful — choosing who lives near you, an app that knows who's around, a home you can leave — each carry a risk if handled carelessly. Safety isn't a constraint on the vision; it's what lets the vision be trusted. Three places to get it right.

  2. A home you can actually leave

Every strength in this project has a shadow. The freedom to choose neighbours can tip into exclusion. The freedom to move can leave the vulnerable unsupported. The app that connects people can be turned against them. None of these are reasons to abandon the ideas — they're reasons to design them with care.

Getting this part right isn't only the decent thing. It's what makes Belonging defensible to a regulator, credible to an investor, and genuinely safe for the people it most wants to help.

01

Choosing neighbours without discriminating

Prevents: exclusion that's unlawful and indefensible

Selection sits at the heart of Belonging — you choose who lives near you, and that's the point. But it carries a danger that has to be met head-on, because mishandling it could sink the whole project. You cannot lawfully choose residents on protected attributes — race, religion, disability, age, sex, or family and marital status. Anti-discrimination law, in Australia and most countries, forbids exactly that in housing.

And here's a hard but useful truth: in its rawest moments, the source material reaches for the language of types of people — keeping out "scumbags," and worse. That kind of selection isn't just unlawful; it's what regulators, journalists, and decent prospective members will recoil from. But read closely, that language doesn't describe what you actually want. What you're trying to escape isn't a kind of person — it's bad conduct: people who are abusive, dishonest, or contemptuous, who make a shared home unbearable. Conduct is a behaviour, not an identity — and selecting on conduct is both lawful and far more effective, because it targets the real thing.

How it works here

  1. Select on commitments, not categories. Membership rests on agreeing to the charter, contributing, and treating people decently — things anyone, of any background, can meet or fail.
  2. Conduct, shown over a trial. The welcome period from Part C lets people demonstrate how they treat others, rather than being judged on who they are or a first impression.
  3. A plain code of conduct. What isn't tolerated is named clearly — abuse, dishonesty, contempt — so that removal (through Part A's process) is always about what someone does.
  4. Checked against the law. Whatever rules a village writes are reviewed against anti-discrimination law before they're used.

This page describes design intent, not legal advice. Selection and membership rules must be confirmed with a qualified lawyer — see Part F, the legal backbone.

02

A home you can actually leave

Prevents: trapping the people most at risk

One of the most humane observations in the book is that being unable to leave is part of how domestic abuse traps people — and that easy moving could help someone get out. That's true and it matters. But easy exit, on its own, is not safeguarding. Harm is usually preceded by a slow isolation, and the moment of leaving can be the most dangerous of all. So the design has to do more than unlock a door: it has to notice, support, and never leave anyone to face it alone.

How it works here

  1. Somewhere to turn. Trained, trusted contacts within the community whom anyone can reach quietly — a first, safe point of support that connects onward to professional services.
  2. Connection as protection. Because the whole community is built against isolation (Parts B and D), the slow cutting-off that often precedes harm is harder to hide and easier to interrupt early.
  3. A real, supported way out. The freedom to move means leaving never costs someone their home or savings — and it's paired with support, not left as a door someone must walk through alone.
  4. Professional hands, always. The community's role is to notice and connect — never to manage danger itself. Clear, current routes to specialist domestic-violence and crisis services are part of the design.
03

An app that protects, not watches

Prevents: a connection tool turned into surveillance

The app is central to the vision — it knows who's nearby, who's "active," what people are doing, and where. That's exactly what makes it useful, and exactly what makes it dangerous. The same feature that helps you find a neighbour to share a meal could help someone track a person they shouldn't. A single breach, or one bad actor, can turn a connection tool into a surveillance tool.

This matters doubly for Belonging, because it exists to help people who've often felt watched, exposed, or unsafe. A tool that made them feel monitored in their own home would defeat its own purpose — and privacy law, and any investor's lawyers, will require this be built in from the start, never bolted on later.

How it works here

  1. Collect as little as possible. The app gathers only what a feature genuinely needs, and keeps it only as long as needed — data minimisation as the default, not the exception.
  2. An easy "invisible" mode. Anyone can go fully unseen with one tap, any time, no explanation — the book's own instinct that some days you don't want to be found, made reliable.
  3. Consent for each kind of sharing. Location, activity, and presence are shared only with explicit, revocable permission — and never on by default.
  4. Built to be secure. Strong security and protection against misuse are foundational, because the cost of getting this wrong falls first on the most vulnerable members.

Safety here isn't the part that waters the vision down — it's the part that earns the right to attempt it. A community that selects on conduct rather than identity, that catches the people slipping through the cracks, and that builds a tool which serves people instead of exposing them, is one that deserves the trust it's asking for.

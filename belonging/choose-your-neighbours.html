The core idea

Choose your
neighbours

Instead of finding a place and hoping about who's nearby, you choose the people you live around — and there's always open space kept free, so the friends you want can move in, and so you can move too, whenever you like. You're not assigned a community. You build one.

The most important thing in life isn't the house. It's the people around it.

We choose almost everything in modern life — our work, our phone, our holidays — except the one thing that shapes us most: who we live beside. We pick a home for its kitchen and its price, and simply accept whoever happens to be next door. Then we wonder why a street full of people can feel so lonely.

Belonging turns that around. The home matters, but it comes second. First you choose your people — and the whole place is built to make that choice real and keep it possible for the rest of your life.

How it works

Four moving parts, working together.

  1. You choose, by application

    Through a simple social app, you ask to live near the people you want around you — friends, or people whose ideas and energy you share. Groups can choose other groups. You're placed on a map close to the people you chose, rather than wherever a vacancy happens to be.

  2. There's always room kept free

    A share of space is deliberately held open — used flexibly in the meantime, but able to be made available quickly. So when a friend wants to join you, or when you want to move closer to someone, there's actually somewhere to go. This is the piece every other model is missing.

  3. You can move, freely

    Because you own your home and can sell it back at a fair formula price (see Homes), moving isn't a financial trap. You can shift to be near new friends, or away from friction, without losing your home or your savings.

  4. A map, placed by who you know

    A nearest-neighbours approach positions people near the friends and groups they chose. You see where people and groups are, and pick where you'd like to be — so the layout of the place reflects real bonds, not the accident of who signed a lease first.

"Isn't this just cohousing?"

It sounds similar. The difference is small to describe and enormous to live in.

Cohousing & co-ops
Good models — but they fill vacancies as they come, and if there's friction someone is asked to leave. There's no open space kept free for the people you want to move in.
Eco-villages
Often built around a lifestyle, and frequently just rentals or premium homes with solar attached. The "choose your people" mechanic isn't the point.
Religious & intentional communities
You join for a shared belief and can be removed for not keeping it. You don't really choose the people — and you can be bound to a creed. Belonging never binds your beliefs (see Governing ourselves).
Belonging
You choose the people. Space stays open so friends can join. You can move when you want. No rent, no creed. That combination, as far as we can find, has not existed before.

These other models are genuinely good. The point isn't that they're bad — it's that none of them keeps space open so you can gather the people you'd actually choose, and let everyone move freely. That single difference is what turns proximity into belonging.

Seeing a friend once a month is a few good hours. Living among people you chose is a different thing entirely — they're there on the stairwell, in the shared meal, in the work you do together, woven into ordinary days. That's the quiet, durable kind of belonging this is built to make ordinary.

Why this works, and why "chosen" is the word that matters, is laid out in Why it works on people. What keeps it fair and un-capturable over time is in Governing ourselves.

The plan in short

  • People choose their neighbours. That is the whole idea; everything else is supplementary.
  • Homes that are paid off, not rented. A person pays off a room or an apartment, whichever fits their budget, at about 30% of their income, on the home's original value plus inflation. Someone who starts with a room can upgrade to an apartment later.
  • Some homes are always kept free, so a group of friends can keep growing and anyone can move at any time. A move is a name change on the papers, not a sale. A custom AI system does the matching and the paperwork; people make the choices.
  • A cheap addition: a work app for local jobs. People switch themselves to "available" and take local casual work whenever they like, often with their friends. Work and things to do exist because the people around them are there.
  • Repayments buy the next homes. The money goes round. If the plan fails, the property is still there, and the money comes back out of it.

What I am asking the program for

  1. Fund or part-fund one pilot: a converted building, a caravan park, or a cluster of cheap houses in one town, where people choose their neighbours and pay off their homes. If the program's flexible funding cannot carry it, I ask you to name the pathway in government that can, and to put this plan to it.
  2. Fund the work app as a community-level initiative. It is cheap, because it is an addition to the matching app I have already built and run, and it is the part that gives people something to do the week they arrive.
  3. Fund the building plan, so that building the homes is the first work. New apartments and rooms are built by the people who will live there and by residents who want building work, whenever they like, through the work app, with a skilled supervisor checking the work. The first people to move in can be the ones interested in that work: they build the next apartments while paying off their own. The fronted costs of each home (the slab, licensed trades and permits, about $15,000 a home in my Riverland case) are repaid at 30% of income, so the money comes back and builds again.
  4. In allocations and transfers, let a tenant ask to be near their people. It costs nothing and it is the one thing the current model never asks.
  5. Fund the organiser role at a human scale: one person for every hundred or so residents, chosen by them, who knows them and can act. Your model already names "local leaders and community advocates"; this is that role, funded.
  6. Use the published work. All of it is licensed for government and funded partners to use with credit, and I offer my services to design the pilot and the app.

How the money works

What is bought. Cheap existing property, so the money goes into homes rather than margins: a hostel, hotel or monastery that converts to owned rooms; a caravan park; a street of cheap houses bought as they come up; a half-empty town; or land with common mains where owners finish their own homes. Rough entry costs from my plan: a caravan park $1–3 million or a lease; a hostel or hotel $300,000–2 million; rural or town-edge houses $50,000–150,000 each.

What residents pay. A room or an apartment is sold at what it cost (with a modest margin where there is a purchase, about 20%), or paid off over time at original value plus inflation. Payments are about 30% of income. Swapping homes costs only the difference in value. People on low incomes own a room and pay it off instead of renting forever.

Where the money goes. Repayments go back into buying or building the next homes, so the fund perpetuates itself. Unpaid homes are resold. Growth pauses by itself when no newcomers arrive. A share of homes is held free as a float; this is what makes every move possible, and it is the feature no other housing model has.

The failure clause, up front. If the community side stalls, what is left is ordinary property in an affordable area: it is sold and the money returned, or the funder keeps the homes. The downside is real estate; the upside is a model that repeats.

A costed South Australian case. I have costed a version for the Riverland, where land is cheap and the towns already have power, water, schools, shops and work: about $1 million for a bare-minimum pilot of self-built homes on land held in trust, or $2 million for the version I recommend, which revives a cluster of eight to ten cheap existing houses so people move in within the first year, with self-build plots beside them and the repayments already turning. It is at chooseneighbors.com/cheapest-room-insa/

What I bring. $50,000 and more of my own savings and part of my future income; my labour, building on commission and organising for free; the working matching app at chooseneighbors.com; and thirty years of design, all published. I am looking for a loan or funding for the rest, and how much I can borrow is still to be worked out.

The work app: the cheap addition

Where most of us live, everything around us is done by strangers from outside the area, who go home afterwards: the bins, the repairs, the shops, the council. There is nothing to do locally with the people you know, and no reason to step outside. The work app changes that.

  • A resident switches to "available" on their phone and lists what they can do. Local jobs, casual work, the upkeep of the place, building the next homes, errands and projects appear, and the person takes what they want, when they want. No applications, no rosters.
  • Friends can take jobs together. The organiser can suggest work to people and check that it is done well.
  • Employers and the community post work to the same list. A local business that hires residents pays less rent in the place; one that hires outsiders pays more. The lease money runs the place.
  • The point is not a job board. It is that there is something to do because the people around you are there, and that a person can earn without giving up their life to a schedule set by strangers.

It is cheap because the matching app already exists; this is a feature on it, not a new system. I will build it myself. It can run in a pilot of twenty homes, and it can run in an existing social-housing street tomorrow.

How moving works

  • You tell the system who you want to live near: people, or a named group-area. It offers you a free room or apartment near them.
  • Some homes are always kept free, so a group of friends can keep growing and anyone can move at any time, to other people or another area.
  • A move is a name change on the papers, not a sale. If the new home is worth more, you pay only the difference.
  • Upgrading: say you want an apartment instead of a room, and the system watches for one near your people.
  • Block someone and the system moves you apart. If you share friends, your friends decide.
  • Nothing happens without your yes. Moving is always a choice; anyone can stay where they are.

Every piece of this already exists somewhere: land trusts, cohousing, cost-rent co-ops, matching software (chooseneighbors.com/the-fragments.html). The assembly is the new part, and the matching that used to make it impractical is now something software and AI can run.

Why it is needed

Isolation. Your own page says it: half of Australians are lonely, isolated, or both, and the harm is on a par with smoking. A program that lifts people towards groups and services they travel to on a schedule helps, but it does not change what is around a person every day. The default life most people are left with is being alone, without community where they live. Homes near chosen people, and work because those people are around, change the default.

Housing as a commodity. Land and homes in Australia are treated as an asset that must never go down. I saved for years while the cheapest homes rose around 20% a year and my term deposit paid 5%. Property prices are left out of the inflation figures because a home is counted as an investment, not a cost of living. Henry George wrote in 1879 that speculation in land "prevents labour from employing itself", and it still describes what I see. The connection to loneliness is direct: when a home is an investment, nobody on an ordinary income can move to be near their people, every move is taxed, and social housing allocates by wait list and scatters people among strangers for life. A loneliness program working inside a housing system built to inflate will keep refilling the same pool of lonely people. This plan takes land and homes out of that system: owned at cost, resold at cost plus inflation, moved by name change.

Representation at a human scale. In my district there is one elected representative for about 60,000 people; below that there is no one whose job it is to know a few hundred people and act on what they say. People stop raising problems, because there is nowhere for them to go. One organiser for every 150 people at most, chosen by the residents and part of the place, is the answer in this plan, and it is the role your model already wants to "activate". It is also why it matters that you asked: this consultation is the first time anyone in government has asked me. Build the asking into the program, through the organiser, so it does not end with this survey.
